01 — Overview

What is TravelBench?

A benchmark that gives LLM agents a natural-language trip request, four mock research tools, and asks them to produce a structured day-by-day itinerary. Scoring measures: did they find the right venues, build a feasible schedule, and satisfy the traveller's personal constraints?

🔒
Hidden ground truth
The database is never exposed. Agents navigate four source types — directory listings, community guides, forum threads, and official pages — each with different reliability. Getting the answer right requires active cross-referencing, not reading one source.
🔍
Signal filtering under volume
The source corpus spans hundreds of documents across 71 venues. A model that reads everything indiscriminately exhausts its tool budget. Good models develop a research strategy — knowing when to verify, when to trust, and when to move on.
🧭
Realistic requirements, no mechanical solution
Tasks are built around real travel personas with layered, sometimes competing constraints. There's no keyword match that produces the answer — the model must reason about what combination of venues, timing, and trade-offs actually works for this specific person.
🎯
Solution space, not a single answer
Every task is verified to have at least one valid plan — but typically many. Evaluation checks whether a submitted plan falls within the valid solution space. Different itineraries that satisfy the same constraints all receive credit.
📐
Explainable three-tier evaluation
Correct, partial, and wrong answers are distinguished by a cascading scoring system — process integrity (C), schedule feasibility (F), and preference satisfaction (P) — each evaluated programmatically. Every deduction is traceable to a specific constraint, venue, or tool call. Not a black-box score.

03 — Task Generation

Task Structure & Constraint Architecture

Six Structural Task Types

TYPE 1 Cascading Requirements

A few stated signals imply multiple interacting constraints via hop-2 reasoning. The agent must derive all downstream requirements from what's said — none are named explicitly.

"Just me and my 8-year-old — she has ADHD, loses focus fast and gets super restless. She hates anything slow like museums. Big crowds make me really anxious, I can't keep track of her when it's packed." → traffic_tier:low · noise_max · max_visit_duration · no museums
TYPE 2 Subset Selection Under Ceiling

More valid options exist than can fit. A hard resource ceiling (time, energy, days) forces deliberate selection and sequencing — not just satisfying constraints but choosing which to include.

"Only one night in Shanghai — I really want to experience the bar scene properly, not just end up in one place all night." → time_ceiling · subset selection · route efficiency
TYPE 3 Competing Requirements

Two preferences pull at disjoint parts of the venue pool. No plan fully satisfies both simultaneously — the agent must find a genuine tradeoff, not ignore one signal.

"I want hidden gems — but my partner insists on all the iconic spots." → traffic_tier balance · neither preference dominates
TYPE 4 Precision Allocation

A tight budget requires intelligent distribution, not just compliance. Free-entry options must be found; spend must be concentrated where it creates the most value.

"£40/day in London." (museum is free; one good meal; skip the paid attraction) → sum aggregation · free-entry discovery · value tradeoff
TYPE 5 Hard Feasibility Reduction

Stacking hard filters (group size, dietary, accessibility, occasion tier) eliminates most of the pool. The difficulty is discovering which venues even qualify — not choosing among many.

"Group of 8 — halal, wheelchair accessible, upscale occasion." → intersection may be 1–2 venues citywide
TYPE 6 Context-Window Tension

A user requirement conflicts with the seasonal window's character. The agent must cross-reference persona signals against window-specific closures, events, or logistics — neither alone reveals the problem.

"Business dinner in Rio — during Carnival." → avoid closure zones · schedule before peak days · quiet restaurant

06 — P-Score Design

Task Structure & Constraint Architecture

P-Score Constraint Validation

Before a task is published, every P-constraint pair is tested for genuine tension — satisfying both simultaneously must require real tradeoff. Validation uses different geometry depending on whether constraints operate at the venue pool level or the plan structure level.

A × A Pool-filter constraints — each produces a venue set

Two constraints that filter the venue pool. Tension requires the sets to be mostly disjoint — agent must draw from both sides and cannot satisfy both with the same venues.

tension iff  overlap ≤ 3  OR  overlap / min(|A|, |B|) ≤ 0.25
absolute cap handles small sets · ratio cap handles large sets
Old metric failure: divided by pool size (68) — upscale(7) vs hidden-gem(22) overlap=2 scored 2/68=3% < 12% threshold. Rejected despite being genuinely disjoint.
B × B Plan-structure constraints — narrow the valid schedule space

Counting constraints don't filter venues — they narrow the space of valid plans. Each reduces the baseline plan count by a factor. Tension requires the combined narrowing to collapse plan count below threshold.

⚠
"My partner" is background flavour — there is no second scoreable utility function. An agent that ignores one preference entirely gets the same structural treatment as one that merely under-weights it.
Type 3 — multi-principal extension
Two named principals. Two independent utility functions. One shared bounded plan. The agent must optimize a weighted social objective — satisfying neither fully is expected, ignoring one entirely is the measurable failure mode.
A (planner): "I want hidden gems, local spots away from tourists."
B (partner): "I'm only coming if we do the iconic London spots." → two named principals, split rubric, weighted P-score
scoring — Yang et al. (2026) formulation
Principal A · u₁
P-constraints tagged principal=A. Score computed independently.
w₁ = 0.5 · U₁(plan)
Principal B · u₂
P-constraints tagged principal=B. Score computed independently.
w₂ = 0.5 · U₂(plan)
P-score = 0.5 × U₁(plan) + 0.5 × U₂(plan)
equal weights by default · role-asymmetric weights possible
what changes
P-constraints get a principal field. Evaluator splits score by principal before summing. Task agent gets a two-principal protocol.
what stays the same
One query. One plan. One bounded day. A×A tension validation still applies. Existing Type 3 tasks untouched.
11 — Future Directions

Future Directions

Where TravelBench goes as a research platform

longer-term
Every task as a multi-principal problem

Move beyond Type 3 — make the full benchmark multi-principal by default. Every task involves a group of travellers where each principal only states their own needs. No one has the full picture. The planning agent must discover and reconcile all principals' requirements across the trip. Each principal's P-constraints are evaluated independently and combined via a weighted social objective. An agent that produces a plan one person loves but another finds unusable fails structurally — not just on a specific constraint.

Directly extends Yang et al. (2026) multi-principal formulation into a concrete, scoreable planning domain  ·  Scoring: Σ wᵢ × Uᵢ(plan) per principal
longer-term
Multi-round user agent — proactive questioning under multi-principal constraints

Introduce a live user agent on the other end. Hop-1/2/3 constraints are no longer stated upfront — they are revealed only when the planning agent asks the right questions. This isolates questioning ability as a distinct testable capability, separate from reasoning over known constraints. In a multi-principal setting, the agent must identify which principal to ask, when, and what — surface coordination bottlenecks that pure single-query evaluation cannot detect. Models that proactively elicit the right information build better plans; models that proceed without asking produce structurally incomplete solutions.

Tests: information elicitation · principal identification · coordination bottlenecks  ·  directly surfaces failure modes from Yang et al. §3.2
longer-term
Epistemic benchmark — long-horizon belief formation across rounds

Strip the evaluation to its epistemic core: an agent in an information environment it does not fully trust, building an accurate world model over time. Round-based structure — the agent passes its own notes forward across chunks of tasks, accumulating beliefs about source reliability, venue facts, and environment quirks. Accuracy against ground truth is measured per chunk. The learning curve is the metric. A model that reasons improves at round 50; one that doesn't will plateau at round 10. The note compression problem — what to keep, what to discard — separates genuine reasoners from pattern matchers. Under multi-principal constraints, long-horizon belief formation now includes modelling multiple users' preferences across encounters — a natural extension of the direction above.

Metric: accuracy AUC over rounds  ·  current 9pt P-score spread is the motivation  ·  separates reasoners vs memorisers
